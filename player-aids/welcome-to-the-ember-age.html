Welcome to the Ember Age

A letter to you, before our first game

You know a little Star Wars. Maybe you've seen a movie or two: glowing swords, a villain in a black mask, a farm kid who saves the galaxy. That's plenty. Honestly, it's more than most people in our story know — and that's the whole idea, so let me explain.

The short version

Our game takes place in the Star Wars universe, but about nine hundred years before any of the movies. Long before Darth Vader, there was a war between the Jedi — the famous warrior-monks with the glowing swords — and their dark counterparts, the Sith. It went on for a thousand years, and it ended in a single afternoon, when a weapon went off that killed almost every Jedi and every Sith at once.

The galaxy won that war. And then, slowly, it lost everything else.

Not in an explosion. Nothing dramatic. Governments just... stopped paying for things. The network that connected every world went quiet, one relay at a time, like streetlights burning out on a road nobody drives anymore. Money stopped being good two star systems over. Long-distance travel became rare, then precious, then almost mythical. Ninety years later — where our story starts — the galaxy is ten thousand islands of people who each remember a different version of how it used to be, and the old folks who actually lived through the war are dying, taking the truth with them.

Nobody broke the galaxy.
Everyone just stopped holding it up.

Where you come in

Between the stars there are beacons — think of them as lighthouses for space travel. Most of them went dark decades ago. Your characters are a small crew with an old, much-loved, slightly-held-together-with-hope ship, and you do the thing almost nobody does anymore: you travel. You find the dark lighthouses and turn them back on.

Every beacon you relight reconnects worlds that haven't heard from each other in fifty years. You'll be the first visitors in living memory. Sometimes you'll be greeted like the sunrise. Sometimes the town went strange in the dark, and you're the ones who get to find out how. And because a reconnected galaxy is worth a fortune — and power — everyone with a flag or a fortune wants to decide what gets rebuilt, and whose name goes on it. They'll all want to hire you. Choosing whose money you take is most of the game.

The magic is coming back

The Force — the invisible power the Jedi used — mostly went quiet after the war. For two generations, almost no children were born with the gift. Now, suddenly, they are, and nobody knows why. There are no schools for them, no wise old teachers, and most ordinary people are afraid of them, because the only thing anyone remembers about magic is that it once burned the galaxy down.

One of you can play someone with this gift, if you like — someone figuring out something enormous with no instructions. Either way, in our first game you're going to find a twelve-year-old stowaway asleep in your cargo hold, dreaming of a place she's never been. What your crew decides to do about her will matter more than any battle.

Why knowing "only the basics" is perfect

Here's the secret: the galaxy has forgotten Star Wars too. Your character has never seen a lightsaber. Nobody you meet has met a Jedi. The war has passed out of living memory into campfire story, and half the stories are wrong. So when we find a piece of the real past — an old journal, a locked temple, a machine that still remembers — you and your character will be discovering it at the same moment, together. The friend at our table who has read every Star Wars book ever printed has no advantage over you. I promise.

What playing actually looks like

If you've never played a tabletop roleplaying game: we sit around a table, I describe a situation — the dock officer wants a bribe you can't afford, and your fuel is on the far side of him — and you tell me what your character does. Talk your way past him, sneak around him, offer him something stranger than money: your call. When the outcome is uncertain, you roll dice.

The dice in this game are special, and honestly they're the best part. They don't just say yes or no. They answer two questions at once: did it work? and what else happened? So you might fail to fix the engine but discover the letter someone hid inside it forty years ago. You might succeed brilliantly, and loudly, at three in the morning, in a port where everyone now knows your name. The dice hand us little twists, and we make up what they mean together. There's a one-page cheat sheet for this, and I'll walk you through your first rolls at the table.

Who you might play

  • The pilot who inherited the ship — and every debt attached to it.
  • The trader who can say "trust me" in five dying languages.
  • The mechanic who has never once bought a part new.
  • The scholar hunting the true story of the war, one page at a time.
  • The bodyguard whose contract outlived the person who signed it.
  • The quiet one who dreams true, and hasn't told anybody.

Whoever you make, they'll start with one complication — a debt, a promise, a person who knows something about them. It's a broke galaxy; everyone owes somebody. That complication is what makes your character a person instead of a hero statue, and I'll build stories out of it, kindly.

Three promises

  • This is a game about fixing things. There will be danger and the occasional firefight, but the heart of it is repair — lights coming back on, strangers becoming neighbors, hope with rust on it.
  • Your choices are the plot. The galaxy is deciding what it becomes next, and nobody — truly nobody — has more say in that than your crew. There is no script you can ruin.
  • No homework. Ever. Everything you need will be at the table. If you're curious there's more to read, but the player who shows up with nothing but curiosity is fully equipped.

Five words you'll hear

Beacon
A lighthouse between the stars. Dark ones can be relit. That's you.
Wayfinder
What people call crews like yours — part explorer, part diplomat, part treasure hunter.
Rutter
A hand-annotated travel journal of old space routes. Often worth more than the ship carrying it.
Scrip
Local money. Nobody's money is good everywhere anymore, which makes favors the real currency.
The Withering
What everyone calls the last ninety years — the long, slow going-out of the lights.

What to bring

A snack to share, if you like. And one idea — even half of one — for a person you'd want standing next to you when the lights come back on. We'll build everything else together at the table.

See you at the first session. The sky's about to change.
